# Script v1 — "Answer First"

Narration word budget: ~600 words ≈ 3:45 at Kokoro's pace plus pauses. Current count is at the
bottom. Each scene = one Remotion component + one narration clip. Scene IDs are stable; the
timeline is the ordered list in `src/timeline.ts`.

Legend: `NARR` narration · `TEXT` on-screen text · `VIS` visual direction ·
`[CHECK: firm-internal]` director must verify · `[FACT n]` dossier reference.

Running example (used in 05, 06, 07, 08): a pricing recommendation.
> **Answer:** Raise the core product's price 8% next quarter.
> **Supports (3Cs, MECE):** Customers will accept it · Competitors have already moved · The company needs the margin.
> **Evidence under "Customers":** retention 94% · willingness-to-pay survey · churn after last increase < 1%.

## 01 · hook (~25 s)

NARR: You learned half of this in ninth grade. Thesis. Three supporting paragraphs. Conclusion.
Then you went to work, and nobody asked you for a five-paragraph essay again.
The best-paid problem solvers in the world never stopped writing them.
They just call it something else.

TEXT: Lined notebook paper. A hand-set five-paragraph outline draws itself: THESIS / 1 / 2 / 3 /
CONCLUSION. On "never stopped," the outline rotates and snaps into a pyramid silhouette.
VIS: Cold open, no title. Warm paper, ink. Motion is slow and deliberate. Last beat holds on the
pyramid, then cuts to black.

## 02 · title (~5 s)

TEXT: ANSWER FIRST · Barbara Minto and the Pyramid Principle
VIS: Serif display on paper. Thin rule. Grain. No narration.

## 03 · thesis, "slide one" (~18 s)

NARR: The Pyramid Principle is the most durable idea in management consulting.
Simple enough to learn in a day. Hard enough to spend a career mastering.
And it has quietly shaped how the world's largest organizations think, decide, and communicate.
Where it came from. How it works. How far it has spread.

TEXT: Top box: the thesis, condensed. Three boxes draw beneath: ORIGIN · TECHNIQUE · REACH.
VIS: This *is* the film's slide one. Lay it out like a clean deck slide. The three boxes become
the chapter markers; each chapter opens by highlighting its box.

## 04 · origin (~45 s)  [FACT B1–B13]

NARR: Barbara Minto was working in Cleveland, without a college degree, when she passed the
entrance exam for Harvard Business School.  [FACT B4, conf. B]
Class of 1963. Eight women in a class of six hundred.
McKinsey hired her that year. Its first woman MBA. Her word for it: the experiment.
In 1966 the firm sent her to London to fix the writing of its European consultants.
She found something else.
"The problem was the thinking, not the language."
When the 1973 oil crisis cut the London office, she stayed. And she started teaching everyone else.
"That," she says, "continues to be the story of my life."

TEXT: A timeline rail: 1963 · 1966 · 1973. Quotes set large in serif when spoken.
VIS: Portrait of Minto, slow Ken Burns, grain. Cleveland → London as two typographic
place-marks on the rail. The quote "The problem was the thinking, not the language" gets the
frame to itself. Chapter ends on her quote, held.

## 05 · technique: the rules (~40 s)  [FACT M1–M6]

NARR: Here is the technique. Start with the answer. Not the background. Not the journey. The answer.
Beneath it, the reasons. Beneath those, the evidence.
Three rules hold it up.
Every box summarizes the boxes below it.
Every level answers the question the level above raises: why? or how?
And the boxes in a group are the same kind of idea, in deliberate order, with no overlaps and no gaps.
Minto named that last test. Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. MECE.  [CHECK: firm-internal — pronunciation "meece" vs "mee-see"]
To open, give the reader a situation they agree with, a complication, and the question it raises.
Then the answer.

TEXT: The pricing pyramid builds: answer box → three reason boxes → evidence row.
Rule 1: brackets drawn under each parent. Rule 2: "WHY?" floats between levels.
Rule 3: the three boxes slide to overlap, then separate; a gap opens and a box fills it.
"MECE" spelled out letter by letter. SCQA: four words stack in the margin.
VIS: All primitives. Boxes are hairline-stroked rectangles on paper, text inside. Overlap/gap
animation is the visual explanation of MECE; keep it clean and literal.

## 06 · technique: every box is a pyramid (~30 s)  [the signature]

NARR: Now the part that makes it powerful.
Every box is the top of its own pyramid.
Push into any reason. Same shape: a claim, held up by grouped, ordered support.
Push again. Same shape.
You stop when the bottom is a fact. Something you can point to, not something you have to argue.
The rule never changes as you go down.
Learn it once, and it works at any size. A sentence. A slide. A year-long problem.

TEXT: "Every box is the top of its own pyramid."
VIS: Camera pushes into the "Customers" box; it fills the frame and reveals its own
answer + three supports. Push into one of those; same shape; the bottom row is evidence
(numbers). Then pull all the way out to show the whole tree at once, three levels deep.
On "a sentence, a slide, a year-long problem," the tree scales down, up, and way up.

## 07 · technique: the deck (~30 s)  [CHECK: firm-internal — the whole scene is the director's account]

NARR: In consulting, the pyramid usually arrives as a deck.
One slide with the answer and, usually, three supporting points.
Behind it, a section for each point. And each section opens with its own answer and its own supports.
In practice, most of that ends up in the appendix.
What the client sees is five slides. The answer. Three supports. Next steps.

TEXT: A stack of slide cards. Slide 1: answer + three bullets. Three sections fan out behind it,
each with its own mini answer slide. The sections collapse into an "Appendix" stack; five cards
remain: 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5.
VIS: Slides are plain cards with the pricing content. No logos. The nesting (section opens with
its own answer) is the recursion again, in deck form.

## 08 · applications: the same shape, going in (~40 s)

NARR: That's the pyramid going out. Communication.
The same shape works going in.
Turn every box into a question and you have an issue tree. One big question, split into parts
that don't overlap, split again, until each piece is something one analysis can answer.
Turn the questions into claims about what must be true, and it's a hypothesis tree.
Assign the branches to people, and it's a work plan.
Minto didn't invent breaking problems into parts.
She gave the practice rules, a test, and a name. And she showed that thinking and writing are one discipline.

TEXT: The pricing pyramid flips: every box's text becomes a question ("Should we raise the price?"
→ "Will customers accept it?" …). Label: ISSUE TREE. Questions become claims: HYPOTHESIS TREE.
Names appear on branches: WORK PLAN. Then the tree resolves back into the answer pyramid.
VIS: One continuous morph. The shape never changes; only the text and the label do. That
constancy is the argument.

## 09 · reach (~40 s)  [FACT K5, K6, I1, I2, I6, I7, I9]

NARR: How far has it spread?
Four decades in print. Nine languages.
The major firms teach it to every new consultant, and then teach it to their clients.  [CHECK: firm-internal]
Then the consultants leave.
The three largest strategy firms employ about a hundred thousand people, and most move into industry within a few years.  [CHECK: firm-internal — "most … within a few years"]
McKinsey alone has some seventy thousand alumni. Eighteen of them are sitting Fortune 500 chief executives.  [FACT I6, I7; as of Oct 2025]
They take the pyramid with them.
And Minto still teaches it herself. Two days. Fourteen people at a time.
The idea is simple. The skill is not.

TEXT: Numbers land one at a time, large, serif: 40 YEARS · 9 LANGUAGES · ~100,000 · ~70,000 ·
18 · 2 DAYS · 14 PEOPLE. Small source line under each (Fortune, Oct 2025; barbaraminto.com).
VIS: A diffusion animation: dots leave a cluster of three firm-shapes and settle across a grid of
companies, each dot carrying a tiny pyramid. Ends on "The idea is simple. The skill is not." set
as two lines.

## 10 · reveal (~20 s)

NARR: Now notice what just happened.
This film opened with a situation, a complication, and a question. Then the answer.
Then three supports. Each one opened with an answer of its own.
Five-paragraph essay. Five-slide deck. Pyramid.

TEXT: The film's outline draws itself as a pyramid: ANSWER FIRST at top; ORIGIN · TECHNIQUE ·
REACH beneath; each chapter's own first line beneath those. Push into TECHNIQUE: its three
sub-boxes appear (rules · every box · the deck). Pull out. The pyramid rotates back into the
ninth-grade outline from scene 01, then into five slide cards.
VIS: This is the callback and the payoff. Reuse the exact primitives from 01, 03, and 07.

And read the book. It's short, and it's the one that started it.
